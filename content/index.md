I work at [Armadin](https://www.armadin.com), where I help build agentic swarms for cybersecurity, and the measurement science of figuring out what they can actually do.

I recently graduated from Stanford, where I studied math and ancient history.

## Research

I want to uncover the general principles behind scaling large AI systems: what capabilities emerge, and how we can keep these systems secure and robust.

I like working on concrete problems, such as backdoor detection, isolation, and containment, as well as fuzzier ones: what are the learning dynamics of these systems, and what happens once they outgrow our ability to check them?

I worked on [Archon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15254) (ICML 2025), a framework for test-time compute, in Chris Ré’s HazyResearch, and did research on [robotics](files/robust-imitation-learning-poster.pdf) in Dorsa Sadigh’s ILIAD lab.

## History

I often think about [“What Have The Romans Ever Done For Us?”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cOKR2m1URe8), usually through the lens of social and economic history, and about the limits of institutions more broadly. Recently, I have been thinking about:

- What informal networks let strangers from opposite ends of the ancient world collaborate at scale (e.g. collegia)?
- What are the limits of rigid institutions, and how do they adapt (e.g. bishops taking over the civic roles of magistrates)?
- How do social orders build trust without verification (e.g. Mediterranean trading communities)?
- Why did Rome never industrialize, and why did it underperform at innovation? Why didn’t their patronage networks cultivate more “scientists” in the provinces?

I wrote [[Collegia and Romanization|an essay]] on how collegia, Rome’s voluntary associations, quietly “Romanized” the empire, and provided the structural scaffolding for the early Christian church.
